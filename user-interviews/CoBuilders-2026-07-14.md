## Snapshot
CoBuilders is a founder-led services firm that does not struggle to find opportunities. Their constraint is the opportunity cost of chasing proposals that lose on politics, incumbents, or misread criteria. Lautaro is skeptical that another listing tool changes the underlying behavior, and pushes the hub toward org-level matchmaking, anonymized competitive intelligence, and long-term service agreements with labs over transactional RFPs.

## Core
1. How do you currently find funding opportunities and RFPs? Tools, cadence?
Three layers. First, interpersonal: early relationships with LatAm domain allocators and Argentinian ecosystem devs who keep them posted on new programs. Second, scaled discovery: AI plus manual scraping of forums, and a full-time researcher (a former ecosystem-program domain allocator) who monitors forums. Third and most effective: a top-down founder sale, where Lautaro reaches out to project founders, asks their priorities, and finds the ones their internal team is not resourced to cover. He also reads Sov's Crypto Grant Wire. With 10 to 12 developers and a founder who generates several times the deal flow needed to keep the team placed, discovery is not their bottleneck.
2. Where does the process break down? What costs the most time?
Not finding work. The costly failure mode is investing founder, researcher, and engineering time drafting large proposals that then do not get captured, lost to competition, political disputes, or a misread of the real criteria. He cited a heavily worked proposal they lost, having de-prioritized a large proposal for another ecosystem to pursue it. He noted the wider damage: small self-funded teams that dissolve after losing a big proposal they built on spec.
3. What information is most often missing or wrong in listings?
The signal that a bid is already lost: whether an incumbent is already building it, whether another team has a head start or a champion, and the real selection criteria versus the stated ones. Today that reading-the-room only comes through relationships. He also flagged wasted time when a listing turns out to be a hackathon, accelerator, or startup-funding program rather than an RFP or service opportunity.
4. If a neutral open hub existed, what would it need to do for you to use it weekly?
Lautaro is skeptical that software alone solves what he frames as a behavioral and incentive problem, not an information problem. For weekly use:
- Org-level matchmaking, Tinder style: match providers to ecosystems and labs by capability and preference, not to individual RFPs.
- Anonymized competitive intelligence per opportunity: how many applied, whether incumbents or previously funded teams are in, how many are strong fits, so he can prioritize (they run one proposal at a time).
- A tilt toward service agreements and long-term lab partnerships (service-provider models with roughly one-year engagements) over transactional RFPs. He wants the hub to generate and incentivize provider-to-lab service-agreement calls, since that market is a black box today.
- Help for publishers to draft better requirements (he pointed to an AI PRD-generation tool, name not clearly captured). More valuable than a better-looking Telegram feed.
He warned that a more open, direct hub for unqualified builders would make things worse: publishers bombarded, scammers, more rejected proposals, and a race to the bottom. Gating to qualified builders helps him at the margin (less need to pay a full-time researcher) but does not change the core competition.
5. Which fields actually matter in a listing?
Stack and needs filters; the decision maker's contact (Telegram handle); opportunity type so RFPs are separated from hackathons, accelerators, and startup funding; real eligibility and criteria; and the intelligence signals above (applicant counts, incumbents).
6. Would you contribute data back? What would make it worth doing?
Open in principle, but wary that full public transparency destroys the value by pushing every interaction into the open and killing the private-relationship edge. He wants as much of the decision process surfaced as can be shared without exposing what is really going on: enough intelligence to prioritize, without making every conversation public.

## Professional services specific
1. How do you decide whether an opportunity is worth bidding on? What kills interest?
Hard rule: one proposal at a time (drafting three at once became an operational mess, so the constraint is now a feature). Prioritization runs on relationship strength, market knowledge, and portfolio fit, and would run better with competitive intelligence. What kills interest is evidence an incumbent already owns it. He will not enter a battle he knows he will lose.
2. Which programs are easiest to work with as a repeat applicant, and why?
He prefers permissioned, champion-based ecosystems despite the gatekeeping, because honest champions tell you upfront when you would lose and save the wasted effort. Direct lab relationships beat open DAO RFPs. He values long-running service-provider programs as a long-term-engagement model.
3. When you respond to an RFP, what is usually missing from the posting?
The decision maker's contact, the true selection criteria, and honest signal about incumbents and competition. Often the only public source is an old forum or Medium post, with details scattered.
4. Agent/API, website, or alerts, in what order?
He did not rank these. His implied preference is intelligence and matchmaking over more alerts; he already has alerts (Sov's wire plus an internal Slack channel) and rates a better-UX Telegram clone as low value. What he wants is org-level matchmaking, opportunity intelligence, and requirement-drafting help.

## Top signals for the build
- Build at the org level, matching providers to ecosystems and labs, not just to individual RFPs.
- Surface anonymized opportunity intelligence (applicant count, incumbents, previously funded, count of strong fits) so providers can hedge and prioritize. His strongest product idea.
- Bias toward service agreements and long-term lab partnerships; there is no infrastructure for provider-to-lab matching today.
- Give publishers help drafting requirements (PRD tooling), rather than replicating a Telegram feed with better UX.
- Add opportunity-type filtering (RFP vs hackathon vs accelerator vs startup funding).
- Openness is a risk: an ungated hub invites spam, scammers, and a race to the bottom. Qualification gating matters.
- Macro thesis: as DAO treasuries lose power and labs take ownership, funding shifts to labs buying services. The hub should anticipate a service-agreement future.
