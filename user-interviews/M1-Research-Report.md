# **RFP Hub — Sov’s M1 Research Report**

## **Introduction**

Web3 funding opportunities are spread across application forms, forums, CRM systems, newsletters, spreadsheets, and social channels. Finding a listing is only part of the problem. Programs can remain public after they close, deadlines change without reaching downstream users, and headline budgets reveal little about what an applicant might actually receive. Publishers face the other side of the same problem: they can issue a well-scoped call and still reach the same small network.

RFP Hub proposes an open standard and shared dataset for these opportunities, supported by a read API, publisher workflow, and simple reference interface. The practical question is whether a common record can make funding information easier to maintain and reuse across the systems publishers, applicants, and aggregators already depend on.

This report draws on 18 completed contributions collected through interviews and written responses: five publisher records, nine builder and services-firm records, two researcher records, and two aggregator records. Four of the 18 were self-authored by the research lead from operating roles held at those organizations, leaving 14 independent contributions; the disclosure and its effect on the findings is set out under Research scope. It also uses an exploratory audit of Karma platform data and a review of adjacent standards. The research points to a focused starting point for the hub, along with several larger opportunities that merit testing later.

## **Executive summary**

RFP Hub should start by making the underlying opportunity record dependable. Status, deadline, source, eligibility, likely award size, and application route were the details respondents most often needed to verify. Publishers want to keep their existing systems, while aggregators need stable records they can synchronize without repeatedly checking and rematching the same listings. That makes the hub most useful as shared infrastructure: one maintained dataset that can feed an API, a digest, and a simple comparison view.

Discovery was not the same problem for everyone. Applicants with strong networks usually heard about programs through funders, peers, X, Telegram, or newsletters, but still had to work out whether a call was active and worth pursuing. People without those networks could miss calls altogether. Both groups needed clearer eligibility, realistic award ranges, decision timing, evaluation criteria, and a way to ask questions before investing in an application.

The interviews also raised a larger possibility. CoBuilders and Cactus were more interested in lasting relationships with organizations than in a stream of narrow projects. Argot and Revoke wanted funder priorities and builder roadmaps to meet before work was reduced to an RFP. This could lead to an opt-in provider layer built around capabilities, organizational needs, prior work, availability, and preferred engagement type. It is worth testing with service firms and maintainers, but the interviews do not support treating it as a universal marketplace.

For v1, the priority is a public dataset with persistent IDs, canonical sources, lifecycle status, verification and change history, structured eligibility, realistic award ranges, application and decision timing, correction handling, exports, and delta synchronization. Profiles, co-funding, public Q\&A, automated matching, reputation, and agent credentials should be tested separately once the core record works.

## **Research scope**

The study used qualitative research with people who publish, pursue, and redistribute funding opportunities. The 18 completed contributions comprise five publisher records, nine builder and services-firm records, two researcher records, and two aggregator records, collected through live interviews and written responses.

Participants were recruited through Karma, Ethereum Foundation, the research lead’s grants network, and existing ecosystem relationships. The same question spine was used across live interviews and written responses, with prompts adapted for publishers, builders, services firms, researchers, and aggregators. Notes were reviewed for repeated workflow failures, adoption conditions, desired fields, and counter-signals. A repeated observation is treated as convergence, not as a statistically representative vote.

Four of the 18 contributions — CoW Grants, ENS DAO Grants, Uniswap Foundation, and Crypto Grant Wire — were self-authored by the research lead from operating roles held at those organizations. They are recorded in full because they carry operational detail no external respondent could supply, but they are not independent observations, and they are concentrated: three of the five publisher records and one of the two aggregator records come from this source. Fourteen contributions are independent — two publishers, nine builders and services firms, two researchers, and one aggregator — and the convergence claims in this report rest on those. Self-authored records are marked as such in Appendix A and are read as corroboration only.

The sample brings together experienced Ethereum publishers, builders, service firms, researchers, and aggregators who work directly with funding programs. Their responses cover the full path from publishing and discovering opportunities to evaluating, applying, syndicating, and maintaining them. This gives Karma a practical foundation for the initial schema, integration model, and product priorities. The same research approach can support future testing as the hub reaches new communities and user groups.

A separate exploratory audit of Karma’s platform data produced one distribution insight worth preserving: the strongest validated activity clustered around recurring, structured programs rather than open discovery. This suggests that program cadence may matter more than listing volume for sustained participation. The result is directional because activity metrics can be gamed and the manually validated sample was small.

## **Findings**

### **Stale records are the main trust problem**

Cornaro Labs described lifecycle monitoring and expired-listing removal as the largest manual cost in its aggregation work. Namespace called active status the field most likely to be wrong. Revoke found programs that had ended while their forms remained online. Remix described public pages and forms that no longer reflected whether programs were active, who held grant authority, or what scope was current. Cactus valued comprehensive, visibly active data more than additional platform features. The independent researcher supplied the most useful lifecycle vocabulary: forthcoming, open, extended, under review, closed, and awarded.

The Climate Collective interview exposed the same problem from an agent-native workflow. Its agent could find plausible opportunities, but stale source data caused it to return closed rounds and incorrect eligibility. That changes the consequence of staleness. A person may ignore an old listing; an agent may act on it. Machine-readable data is only useful when its status and provenance can be trusted.

The standard should therefore treat every opportunity as a persistent record rather than a replaceable listing. It needs a stable identifier, canonical and alternate source URLs, a current lifecycle state, posted and updated timestamps, and an audit trail of material changes. Status and deadline should carry verification information because they decay faster than descriptive fields. Corrections need an acknowledgement and resolution path so contributors can tell whether their work reached the shared record.

### **Applicants need enough information to decide whether to apply**

For networked respondents, the hardest task was rarely finding another link. Cactus spends hours estimating effort because RFPs often fail to connect scope, duration, and budget. Its clearest proposed remedy was an in-window Q\&A route where publisher answers are visible to every applicant, reducing duplicate clarification and making scoping information part of the opportunity record. CoBuilders loses proposal time when an incumbent, political relationship, or unstated criterion determines the result. Namespace wanted four things quickly: eligibility, realistic award size, deadline, and someone to contact. Revoke’s year-long attempt to inspect every supported chain and approach relevant foundations produced little funding despite substantial effort.

Budget honesty was the strongest specific schema recommendation. Namespace objected to programs advertising a large pool while offering very small individual awards. The zero-to-one builder interview made the same distinction between money announced and money a recipient can actually receive. One researcher treated funding disproportionate to the required work as an immediate reason not to apply. Program budget and realistic per-award range should be separate fields.

Application cost also belongs in the opportunity model. Namespace rejected unnecessarily long applications and detailed milestone budgeting at the first stage. One researcher considered preparation time before deciding to apply and rated typical research calls 4 out of 10 for clarity, citing vague research questions, deliverables, review processes, and evidence standards. Read beside Cactus’s warning about over-specified technical RFPs, the pattern is uneven specification: publishers can prescribe implementation while leaving evaluation and evidence unclear. The institutional researcher preferred a light initial screen that disclosed the number advancing and required substantial work only after that cut. Publishers cannot know final odds before applications arrive, but they can disclose stages, the number advancing, the number funded, and later-stage applicant counts when those figures exist.

Eligibility needs similar treatment. One free-text paragraph cannot reliably represent geography, jurisdiction, entity type, affiliation, sector, ecosystem, applicant stage, consortium requirements, audited-financial requirements, or required partners. The record should also include exclusions and redirects. Uniswap’s distinction between standard grants, security work, incentives, consulting, and infrastructure is useful because it routes applicants rather than merely rejecting them.

### **The hub needs to work with existing systems**

Publishers did not ask for a replacement grants-management product. The two independent publisher records agree on this: EF wants opportunities to flow from Salesforce with minimal maintenance, and DAO Security Fund expects Karma to pull structured RFP data from its own platform. The three self-authored publisher-seat records describe the same architecture in more operational detail — Uniswap through pushes or webhooks from existing pipeline systems, CoW keeping its forum record canonical, ENS needing distinct funding lanes and owners to remain visible even as terms and governance structures change — and are read here as corroboration rather than independent confirmation.

Aggregators expressed the same architecture from the other side. Cornaro, the independent aggregator record, wants the hub as an attributed upstream input, not a competing browse product. The self-authored Crypto Grant Wire workflow needs stable IDs, strict timestamps, source aliases, bulk export, and updated-since queries. Both need to compare the shared registry against their own records without rematching every item by title or URL.

This makes the API the primary integration surface, but it does not eliminate human interfaces. Namespace and the institutional researcher wanted periodic digests. The other researcher wanted a simple surface where several opportunities could be compared. The right product is one canonical data layer with several thin outputs: an open API for programmatic users, a digest for passive discovery, and a plain filterable table for comparison. A rich consumer application is not supported by the research.

Public discovery data should remain open, consistent with the project proposal. The zero-to-one builder interview raised concerns about rogue or fraudulent agents, which are better addressed at the write boundary. Submissions, corrections, and automated applications can carry identity, provenance, rate limits, moderation, and publisher-visible credentials without closing public reads.

### **Many needs appear before an RFP exists**

Several interviews exposed the limits of treating every need as a finished RFP. EF uses wishlists when it knows the area of need but has not specified a project. Argot wants to see ecosystem priorities before deciding which work packages to develop. Revoke argued that conventional RFPs often force maintainers to build adjacent projects rather than improve an existing product; the more useful exchange would let builders publish possible roadmap directions while funders publish needs. ENS requires a program lane, owner, term, decision path, and funding instrument to explain where an applicant belongs. Uniswap separates funder from operator because a foundation may fund a program run by another organization.

The services interviews pushed this further. CoBuilders would rather be matched to an organization or lab with an ongoing need than repeatedly compete for tactical projects, and proposed capability-based matching plus longer service agreements. Cactus described RFPs as a way to enter an ecosystem, with the real value arriving through a durable relationship after the initial engagement. Argot’s request for funder priorities and Revoke’s request for two-way roadmaps point toward the same upstream layer: organizations publish problems and strategic directions; qualified builders and providers publish capabilities, evidence, availability, and possible paths; the system helps both sides identify a fit before the need hardens into a narrow RFP.

The opportunity record could provide the base for a provider-matching pilot. Participating firms and builders could publish their capabilities, prior work, availability, and preferred engagement type, while organizations publish priorities and unresolved needs. Revoke and the institutional researcher offered useful limits: some services work will continue to move through ordinary contract channels, and research funding often depends on established institutional relationships. Any provider layer should therefore be optional and tested first with the groups that asked for it.

Co-funding appeared as a related requirement. DAO Security Fund is organizing security work around a cornerstone funder and conditional pledges from co-funders. Argot and Remix described adjacent matching and co-funding arrangements. The evidence supports representing funding intent, cornerstone funders, conditional pledges, and thresholds. A complete co-funding workflow should wait until a real DAO Security Fund example has been encoded and tested.

Profiles require the same restraint. Remix saw value in reusable qualifications and prior work. Argot can contribute roadmaps, transparency reports, and usage data. The institutional researcher warned that researchers already maintain too many profiles and suggested importing from ORCID or existing publication systems, then asking only whether someone is open to opportunities and whether they want contracts or project funding. An optional import-based profile is plausible. A mandatory profile or services marketplace is not.

### **How people want to use the data**

Namespace, Cactus, and Climate Collective wanted API access because they already work through software and agents. Revoke wanted tailored alerts but planned to keep final decisions manual. The independent researcher preferred a searchable page for comparing opportunities. The institutional researcher wanted a newsletter that would bring relevant calls into an existing routine.

All four approaches can use the same underlying record: an API for programmatic access, alerts and a digest for passive discovery, and a simple table for comparing opportunities. Those are enough for the initial interfaces.

## **What the standard cannot solve alone**

The applicant interviews produced useful ideas that extend beyond an opportunity standard. Capability profiles, provider qualification, organization-level matching, reusable application history, tailored alerts, consortium formation, applicant status feedback, publisher requirement-drafting support, and relationship management could all improve the market around the hub. They should be treated as adjacent product opportunities. The standard can provide the underlying organization, profile, evidence, priority, and opportunity objects, while later pilots determine which workflows users will actually adopt.

Several meetings identified failures that better data can expose but cannot eliminate. Some builders described RFPs where incumbency, private relationships, or unstated preferences appeared to matter more than the published criteria. A hub can make evaluation criteria, applicant Q\&A, decision timing, and incumbency disclosures visible. It cannot make a publisher run a genuinely competitive process. This is a process-legitimacy problem, not a schema defect.

The supply side also needs a reason to maintain data. DAO Security Fund would keep records current if the hub helped attract co-funders and attention. EF wants better reach without a second publishing workflow. Cornaro will contribute when attribution and shared maintenance reduce its own work. The self-authored CoW record reports the same motivation from the publisher seat: distribution to qualified builders. The common incentive is operational value, not data hygiene. The publisher's proposition should therefore be distribution, syndication, co-funder discovery, and reduced duplicate maintenance. 

Revoke identified a deeper funding-instrument problem. Existing products need maintenance, reliability, and performance work, while grants and RFPs frequently reward discrete new builds. That pushes maintainers toward adjacent projects that fit milestone templates rather than improvements their users need. Priorities and roadmap objects can make this mismatch visible, but they do not create a durable maintenance-funding mechanism. 

Verification has a similar boundary. Uniswap described the cost of checking team history, repositories, deployments, on-chain activity, and traction. Remix showed why generic activity metrics can misrepresent online tools, educational products, and services. The Karma audit found that milestone and update counts can remain impressive while underlying product health is weak. The hub should carry claim-specific evidence and verification methods, not collapse them into a universal reputation score. 

Finally, factual lifecycle feedback and subjective reputation need different governance. EF avoids visitor-level tracking. Remix supported applicant-contributed signals but raised the risk of turning private experiences into public claims. Cactus doubted anonymous publisher ratings because respondents can be identified in small processes. The hub can safely collect source changes, response status, and correction evidence. Claims about fairness, payment behavior, or counterparty quality need stronger moderation and should remain outside the core record. 

**A possible future state**

A reliable opportunity registry could support more than publication and discovery. Organizations could share priorities and unresolved needs before they have written an RFP. Builders, maintainers, researchers, and service firms could choose to publish capabilities, prior work, availability, and the kinds of engagements they want. That would give Karma a practical way to test whether better matching produces useful conversations or longer-term relationships.

The same records could improve an active selection process. Public Q\&A would prevent applicants from repeating the same scoping questions. Reusable evidence could reduce duplicate diligence. Review stages, decision dates, applicant counts, and disclosed incumbency would give applicants a clearer view of the process. Co-funding records could show which organizations have committed money and what conditions remain.

After an award, the opportunity ID could link to the selected provider, delivery evidence, outcomes, maintenance needs, and follow-on work. This would create a record of what happened without relying on a single reputation score.

These ideas should remain separate pilots. The first test is still whether RFP Hub can maintain accurate opportunity records and connect them to publisher and aggregator systems. The schema should leave room for organizations, profiles, priorities, relationships, and outcomes so successful pilots can be added without rebuilding the foundation.

## **Implications for the standard**

The v1 standard should concentrate on the opportunity record. Each record should have a persistent ID; opportunity type; publisher, funder, and operator where distinct; canonical and alternate source links; source attribution; current lifecycle; posted, updated, and verified timestamps; application window; deadline type; expected decision and start dates where known; program budget; realistic per-award range; currency and payout mechanism; structured eligibility; exclusions and redirects; hard and soft requirements; required evidence; reporting expectations; evaluation criteria; and a human or machine contact route when available.

The API should support public unauthenticated reads, filtering, search, pagination, sorting, bulk JSON and CSV exports, and updated-since delta queries. Material edits should produce dated change events while the API continues to expose a current consolidated record. This follows the release-and-record logic used by the [Open Contracting Data Standard](https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/primer/releases_and_records/) and directly addresses the stable-ID and synchronization requirements raised by aggregators.

Compatibility should be deliberate. [DAOIP-5](https://github.com/metagov/daostar/discussions/51) separates grant systems, pools, applications, projects, and payouts; RFP Hub can map to those concepts without assuming every opportunity is on-chain. Karma already has an open [DAOstar collaboration issue](https://github.com/metagov/daostar/issues/269), providing an existing implementation thread for that crosswalk. [Schema.org Grant](https://schema.org/Grant) provides a broad vocabulary for funders, amounts, and funded items but lacks the lifecycle and provenance model required here. [Grants.gov](https://www.grants.gov/system-to-system/grantor-system-to-system/web-services/get-full-opportunity-details) separates forecasts, synopses, packages, documents, links, and related opportunities, which supports treating forthcoming calls and application packages as distinct objects. The [ORCID public API](https://info.orcid.org/what-is-orcid/services/public-api/) makes import-based researcher profiles technically feasible.

Wishlists, priorities, program lanes, co-funding, research publication terms, public Q\&A, reusable profiles, and award outcomes should be modeled as extensions or related objects. A future supply-and-demand layer would also need organization needs, provider capabilities, evidence of prior work, qualification state, availability, preferred engagement type, and relationship history. These fields should be designed so the opportunity record can support later matching without making profiles mandatory in v1. Deposit-based freshness incentives, agent credentials, applicant-odds displays, reputation scores, publisher ratings, and automated matching remain experiments. A full services marketplace, autonomous application submission, closed discovery dataset, general-purpose knowledge graph, and broad non-crypto expansion should remain outside v1.

## **Conclusion**

The research supports a focused first version of the RFP Hub. Its immediate job is to keep opportunity records current, attributable, and easy to reuse. Stable IDs, lifecycle status, provenance, eligibility, realistic award information, and synchronization matter more at this stage than a large interface or broad marketplace.

If RFP Hub can make that record dependable, it will have the foundation to test wishlists, co-funding, provider profiles, matching, and outcome tracking. Those additions should earn their place through real use rather than being built into v1 by assumption.
