# Cactus (fmrly ScopeLift) — Interview Summary — 2026-07-15

_Last edited: 2026-07-16T17:19:00.000Z_

Interviewees: Ben and Hugo (Cactus, formerly ScopeLift). Mahesh attended. Interviewed 2026-07-15.

## How they find opportunities
Ben's process was ad hoc (word of mouth, Twitter, referrals from current and former clients), which is why Hugo now owns discovery. Hugo runs an AI pipeline that turns a whitelist of Telegram chats and channels into daily summaries, layered on Sov's newsletter and Telegram channel, crypto Twitter, and a network of contacts built since 2017. He is actively looking for better Twitter sourcing beyond the official API.

## Where the process breaks down
Discovery is not the bottleneck; scoping is. RFPs rarely carry enough detail to estimate level of effort against budget, so each serious opportunity costs hours of preliminary discovery. A $50K grant can be a great or terrible opportunity depending on whether it is a two-week or six-month build, and the RFP usually does not say. AI speeds this up but it cannot be fully automated: the binding constraint is whether the information exists at all, or whether there is access to the people who have it.
Publisher disorganization compounds it. Overspecified RFPs are a failure mode too: if a spec is written to the letter, the design work is already done and the engagement is commoditized.

## Top feature ask
Ben's number one: a transparent Q&A channel with the RFP publisher during the application window, as a default part of the process, with answers visible to all applicants. Not more bureaucracy, just access, so teams are not scoping three-month projects off a doc thrown over the wall.

## What would drive daily use
Hugo: the underlying data, not the platform. It must be comprehensive, exhaustive, and visibly active. Stale directories and outdated spreadsheets are the graveyard to avoid; the best proof of life is grants actually being there.

## Fields and data contribution
Standard fields matter (duration, budget, eligibility), but the data they most want is counterparty quality: are the stakeholders technical, do they pay on time, will they creep scope. They acknowledged this is hard to reduce to a data point. Both were skeptical of anonymous publisher ratings: for a publisher with one RFP, deanonymization of the reviewer is trivial, and rating clients publicly is bad form regardless.

## Vetting and values
They pass on projects that smell like vaporware or overselling, and Ben has regretted the borderline yeses. Values alignment is a filter but not a purity test.

## Preferred interaction
Agent and API interface with clear instructions an agent can follow, plus alerts via email and Telegram.

## Meta: the selection effect
Ben's closing point: when a team posts an RFP, ask why they do not already have a trusted provider. Many RFP processes are effectively decided in advance, with an incumbent favored, and Cactus has lost strong proposals to preexisting relationships. He treats RFPs as low-probability foot-in-the-door plays into ecosystems where they have no footprint yet, with the goal of converting into an ongoing relationship. This echoes CoBuilders' tilt toward long-term service agreements over transactional RFPs. Hugo separately floated the public-good marketplace angle: intermediating supply and demand efficiently, drawing on marketplace precedents from his cybersecurity days.
