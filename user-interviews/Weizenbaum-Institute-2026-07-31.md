## Background

Postdoc at the Weizenbaum Institute in Berlin. Long stint in Paris with Primavera De Filippi researching blockchains and governance. Comes at this from an academic research seat, not a builder or service-provider seat.

Notably, she has **shifted her research focus away from crypto toward data centers** over the past couple of years. That shift is itself one of the more useful findings from the call.

## How she finds funding opportunities

She named a concrete, layered set of channels, worth stating plainly because it differs from the other researcher in the sample:

- **Metagov Slack**, which has a dedicated opportunities channel
- **All Tech Is Human**, as a broader resource spanning technologies rather than a single stack
- **Place-based boards for Berlin**, which list local opportunities
- **Her research institute's own channel**

And the mechanism she singled out as working best:

> A lot of universities and research institutes, like the bigger ones like ours, will have a department that sort of sources grant opportunities for the people working at the institute, which I think works very well.
> 

**An institutional intermediary does her filtering.** No builder in the sample has an equivalent. This reframes the discovery question for the researcher archetype: the consumer of a hub may be the university research office rather than the individual researcher.

She then named what the waste actually is, unprompted:

> The biggest waste of time is sort of like filtering the signal from noise. Because in a lot of cases grants are quite specific. They want something specific.
> 

So for her, **discovery is not broken, filtering is the cost.** That lands her closer to the builders (Cactus, CoBuilders, Cap) than to Sofia.

### Topic switch equals channel switch

The single most structurally interesting thing she said:

> You switch into a different place to look for opportunities, different groups of people. Every topic has their community.
> 

Moving from crypto to data centers meant losing her discovery layer and rebuilding it from scratch. Discovery infrastructure in research is **community-scoped, not researcher-owned.** A researcher who changes subject loses everything they had accumulated.

This is a direct argument for the hub being cross-community rather than crypto-only, and the second independent signal in that direction after Cornaro's EU CORDIS coverage.

### The prestige hierarchy

New to the sample. Academic funding runs a ladder: national funders first (in Germany, the DFG), then European, then larger still. Different grants map to different career stages, and you climb.

> Just like with startups who want to have a certain VC backing them because it implicitly signals certain things, the same goes in academia. Who is funding your work is very important.
> 

**Funder identity is a credential, not just a source of money.** No builder raised this. Uncomfortable implication for the project: a crypto-native funder sits outside the academic ladder entirely, so prestige may be a real barrier to attracting serious academic applicants regardless of how good the listing infrastructure is.

## Interface preference

Unambiguous, and again not what the builders said.

> I don't know if a lot of people feel this way, but I love newsletters.
> 

She does not actively check anywhere. She wants a digest **once a week or twice a month** that hands her the opportunities so she can scan and move on rather than maintaining an item on her task list. Second preference is a website that is "just a table" and explicitly does not need to be fancy.

**She never mentioned an API or an agent.** It did not surface at any point, including when Sov named Claude directly in the question.

The agent/API-first consensus now has three dissenters. Both researchers are among them.

But the two researchers **disagree with each other**: Sofia wants a website because comparison is a surface task, Tara wants a newsletter so she never has to visit a surface at all. What unites them is that neither wants to do any work to receive the information. The real axis is not agent versus human. It is whether the respondent's own work is already programmatic. The API-first group is infrastructure and services people whose workflows are code; the dissenters are people whose workflows are not.

## Profiles

The clearest, most buildable answer anyone has given on the profile question.

Profile fatigue first:

> People don't like making new profiles. I've made a profile on every single sort of platform already. And then it's just like data, privacy, all these things.
> 

Her proposed shape:

- **Pull the expertise from where it already lives.** Google Scholar for the publication record, including non-academic publications where you can add a link. **ORCID** is the system most academics use and is closer to a standard, though she finds it imperfect. A mix of the two.
- The researcher supplies almost nothing: **their name, a flag that they are open to opportunities, and one toggle** distinguishing openness to contracting from looking for project funding.

A concrete spec: for the researcher archetype the profile is an import plus two flags, not a form.

She also drew the contracting/funding line herself:

> In the research space it's less contracting and more funding, like for projects.
> 

She would accept a contracting toggle but it is not her mode. **Second counter-signal to the service-provider hub thesis** after Revoke, with the nuance that she would opt in rather than reject it outright.

## What is missing from RFPs

Her answer was single and emphatic: **the odds.**

She described a call she was approached to apply to, and what she appreciated was the structure:

- A low-effort first-stage screening
- 25 projects selected from that stage
- 15 of those 25 funded

Two distinct things work there. The application effort is **staged**, so the heavy work only happens once you have survived a cut. And the funnel numbers are **published**, so the expected value of that heavy work is calculable.

> What is missing is a realistic estimation of the odds of getting something.
> 

She acknowledged publishers cannot know applicant volume up front, and pointed at the resolution herself: be transparent at the second stage, where applicant count is known and available funding is fixed.

**Third independent hit on application-side cost as a first-class filter**, after Cap (unnecessarily long applications, milestone-based budget breakdowns) and Sofia (application preparation cost enters her decision sequence). Tara is the first to propose the mechanism rather than just name the pain: stage the application and publish the funnel.

## How she applies

At her career stage, applications are **group or consortium efforts by default**. The team pre-exists, skill sets are already known, and it is composed of "people you know." Contracting out for specific gaps happens **after** the award, not during the application.

> In the application phase, it's people you know and you know their skill set.
> 

She expects this to invert with seniority: more senior researchers apply individually and then use the funding to hire teams. At the largest scale, applications go in as consortia of universities and institutions.

**No matchmaking need at application time.** Third data point suggesting the org-level matchmaking ask is specific to services firms (CoBuilders) rather than universal, alongside Revoke's counter-signal on the service-provider hub.

## Her own caveat, and the build risk she named

Volunteered when asked what had been missed:

> Within research, my perspective is a very specific perspective. It depends on which discipline you're asking. It would be very different if you're talking to a physicist or a psychologist. All these disciplines have very different funding landscapes and social norms around what you want and what you don't want. It's super fragmented, which makes it very hard to build such a dashboard, but I love the idea.
> 

**Discipline fragmentation is the named build risk for the researcher archetype.** Two researcher interviews cannot characterize research funding, and she is explicit that a single dashboard across disciplines is hard for reasons of social norms, not just data.

## Artifact she shared

The Big Artist Opportunities List

Offered unprompted as her model of a resource that works, sent in the chat at the end of the call. Crowdsourced by artists, and per her description it carries deadlines, amounts, location, and target eligibility (whether you must be a painter, a digital artist, and so on).

> It's just a Google Sheet. But people put in the deadlines, the amounts, the location, the targets.
> 

Worth pulling into the report as an existing-solution exhibit. It supports two threads at once: that the useful thing is a plain table with the right columns rather than a product, and that the strongest reference cases sit **outside crypto**. Title confirmed; the column list is her description and is not independently verified, since the sheet is client-rendered and did not return contents on fetch.

## Where this sits against the rest of the sample

**Sharpens the discovery finding rather than settling it.** Sofia was the first respondent for whom discovery is genuinely broken. Tara, same archetype, has four or five working channels plus an institutional grants desk. The difference between them does not look like archetype. Sofia is a PhD candidate whose opportunities arrive informally from colleagues; Tara is a postdoc at a well-resourced institute that employs people to source opportunities for staff. **Discovery quality appears to track institutional support rather than personal skill or seniority alone.** A sharper version of the finding than either interview gives on its own, and it should go in the report that way.

**Converges** with: filtering-not-finding as the real cost (Cactus, CoBuilders, Cap); application-side friction as a first-class filter (Cap, Sofia); plain, comprehensive, current data over product features (Cactus, Cornaro).

**Diverges** from: the agent/API-first interface consensus (third dissenter); the service-provider hub thesis (second counter-signal); org-level matchmaking as a general need (third contrary data point).

**Genuinely new:** the institutional grants-sourcing desk as the real discovery layer; topic switch equals total channel switch; funder prestige as a credential; staged applications with published funnel odds; profile-by-import from ORCID and Google Scholar.
